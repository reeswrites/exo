Every notation I have used well started as a compression of something I already
understood. Every notation I used badly was a compression of something I hoped to
understand later. The difference is not in the symbols; it is whether the thing
being compressed had a shape before it had a name.

A card deck is a notation with an opinion. It insists that order is the only
structure worth encoding, and it will happily let you write a program whose meaning
depends on a stack of cardboard staying in one piece. That is not a limitation of
the machine — it is a claim about what matters, made by the medium, on your behalf.

So when I reach for a new way of writing something down, I try to ask what claim the
notation is making before I ask whether it is convenient. Convenience is cheap and
reversible. An unexamined claim about structure will still be shaping my thinking
six months later, long after I have forgotten that I accepted it.

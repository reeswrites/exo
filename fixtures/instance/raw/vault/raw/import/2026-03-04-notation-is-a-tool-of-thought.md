Every notation I have used well started as a compression of something I already
understood, and every notation I used badly was a compression of something I
hoped to understand later. The difference is not in the symbols. It is whether
the thing being compressed had a shape before it had a name.

Punch cards taught me this the hard way. A card deck is a notation with an
opinion: it insists that order is the only structure worth encoding, and it will
happily let you write a program whose meaning depends on a stack of cardboard
staying in one piece. That is not a limitation of the machine. It is a claim
about what matters, made by the medium, on your behalf.

So when I reach for a new way of writing something down, I try to ask what claim
the notation is making before I ask whether it is convenient. Convenience is
cheap and reversible. An unexamined claim about structure will still be shaping
my thinking six months later, long after I have forgotten I accepted it.
